Abstract

Overview of This Review

A graph is a foundational non-linear data structure used to model relationships between connected objects. Graph algorithms allow computational systems to analyse complex networks, perform efficient searches, and evaluate optimal paths. This review covers three fundamental algorithms — BFS, DFS, and Dijkstra's — exploring their mechanics, algorithmic steps, visual behaviours, and theoretical complexities.

Additionally, we analyse real-life applications across social networks and GPS routing, alongside recent research directions including dynamic streaming graphs and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).

BFSDFS DijkstraGNNs Streaming Graphs Graph Neural Networks
📐
What is a Graph? G = (V, E)
A graph G = (V, E) consists of Vertices (nodes) V — points representing entities like cities or servers — and Edges E — links connecting pairs of nodes like highways or friendships. Graph operations primarily centre on traversal (visiting nodes systematically) and pathfinding (finding optimal routes).
🌐
Why Graphs?
While linear data structures store elements sequentially, real-world systems are rarely linear. Internet connections, highway networks, and social interactions are deeply interconnected — and graphs are the natural language for representing these complex relationships.
4. Recent Research Trends

Cutting-Edge Directions

⚡

Dynamic Streaming Graphs

Real-world graphs change constantly — transactions, social updates, sensor data. Incremental algorithms maintain shortest paths in O(log V) time per update without restarting from scratch.

StreamingApache FlinkReal-time
🌐

Distributed Graph Processing

Web-scale graphs (Facebook: 3B nodes, Google: trillions of edges) cannot fit in one machine. Apache Spark GraphX and Google Pregel partition computation across server clusters.

Spark GraphXPregelBig Data

Graph Neural Networks

BFS-style message-passing meets deep learning in GCNs. Applied to drug discovery, fraud detection, protein structure prediction, and recommendation systems.
